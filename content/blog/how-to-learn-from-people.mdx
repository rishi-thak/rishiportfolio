This summer, I interned at a consulting firm called AHEAD. Consulting is inherently people-centric: you need to talk to lots of people, and you need to be good at it. Whether that meant communicating clearly with my manager, breaking technical problems down into plain language for a client, or simply asking colleagues for advice, I found it extremely important to keep honing my skills.

I had TONS of coffee chats. On my first day, I talked to my manager, who pointed me to three other people to chat with. Each of those three pointed me to another three, and you can see where that goes. Some were laid back: we talked about sports, school, the company, or our coworkers. Others were much more formal: their career path, general advice, what they were working on. Along the way, I realized some chats went much better than others.

There are some obvious reasons for this:

- Better preparation before the chat
- Natural overlap in interests or hobbies
- Timing: right after lunch, not first thing in the morning or near the end of the day

But what reasons were there that I wasn't seeing right away? Here, I'll go over some "tricks" I noticed through these coffee chats, and why each one works.

## Start with the basics

### Be human

Of course, the simplest one: Try to connect on a personal level first: hobbies, job function, family, and so on.

### Be curious

To learn, you must WANT to learn. Ask "why" over and over again, and ask the questions that naturally come to you.

### Listen

For a lot of people, it's hard to simply listen. Sometimes I just want to jump in with MY point. Taking a step back and letting the other person linger on their thoughts and build on them is not a bad thing. Slow down, and reply once you've heard the whole story. Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People* has a great section on this.

## Then, ask better questions

### Prepare before the chat

Look at their LinkedIn: their job description, their projects, and so on. See their portfolio or "about me" slide if you can.

Based on what you see, draw some parallels between their experience and what you might want to do in the future. Questions should naturally jump out at you. For example, lots of people move from Tax or Audit to Advisory. If that's something you're curious about, remember it.

### Have a wide breadth of knowledge

This is something my dad heavily emphasized growing up. You should be able to hold a conversation about an EXTREMELY wide range of topics, whether it's the 49ers, GraphRAG, or cars. This seriously helped me make personal connections. Simply staying up to date on what's going on in the world is a huge leg up.

### Disagree

Don't be afraid to offer opposing viewpoints. As you can imagine, across all these coffee chats, quite a few people said something that directly contradicted what someone else had told me. I found it interesting to bring up that opposing viewpoint, like: "I've also heard [viewpoint 2]. What do you think?" Always ask for that feedback. More often than not, you'll uncover a new way to look at viewpoint 2, OR more information on viewpoint 1.

A different view isn't a bad view.

### Keep improving

Let's say you have five coffee chats over the next two weeks. Try not to ask any two people the exact same question. ALWAYS keep refining the questions you ask. Notice the question you asked, how you phrased it, and the response, then adjust the first two to improve the third.

## Asking for advice

With two weeks left at AHEAD, I'd just gotten back from Chicago, where we gave our final intern presentations, and I realized my internship was ending very quickly. I had a long list of people I'd had coffee chats with, and many I wanted to circle back with. So toward the end of my internship, I scheduled "advice chats" with the people I felt I'd really connected with. In these chats, I'd recap how the summer went, talk about my future goals, pick their brains, then ask an extremely general question: "Do you have any advice for me moving forward?"

This usually got one of two responses: "trust your gut" or "never give up." Both are good advice, but I realized I'd been looking for something hyperspecific all along: something I could actually go and act on.

## The issue with asking general questions

- It may feel like casting a broad net, or giving the other person an open floor to say what they need to say, but it usually leads to a general answer.
- General question = general answer.
- Like the last tip: keep improving. Ask questions you think will get you more specific answers. Find a balance between keeping questions unbiased (not fishing for a specific answer) and not asking questions that invite general answers.

**Next time, instead of "any advice?", I'd ask: "How do you think I should approach recruiting in the future? How did you do it?"**
